Autobiography · professional edition

I somehow worked around almost the whole plant.

The short version is that I have spent years ending up where people, product, paperwork, and systems collide. The longer version starts well before cannabis — in North Philadelphia, grocery front ends, restaurant floors, college classrooms, and one extremely consequential move west.

This is not the résumé. It is the route that explains the résumé: why inventory became a strength, why I care about records, why handoffs bother me when they are sloppy, and why ClearLine eventually made sense as something to build.

01 · Before the plant

My first systems were grocery stores.

North Philadelphia · 2000s–2010s

My first paid work was at Oteri’s Bakery & Jewelry in North Philadelphia. Then came ShopRite #402 at Oxford & Levick, starting Christmas week of 2007, followed by Food Basics and years of front-end, service, and hospitality work.

I did not have language for “operations” yet. I just knew I liked understanding how the whole thing moved: who needed to be where, which queue was about to break, what needed replenishing, where the bottleneck was, and how to keep the work from falling on the floor.

02 · The expected route

Penn State was supposed to lead somewhere more conventional.

Penn State Abington · 2015–2016 transition

College sharpened the instinct I already had. Corporate communication and marketing gave me a formal language for organizations, messaging, power, and systems; Dr. Brinkert’s lessons about systems and grit stayed with me long after the classroom.

The transition out of school was not clean. A family and housing rupture near the end of college made the neat “degree → first corporate job → ladder” story much less neat. I still expected to build a conventional career. The conventional career did not appear particularly interested in cooperating.

03 · West

So I bought the ticket.

Philadelphia → Denver · May 2016

A college friend pitched legal cannabis in Denver. It sounded impulsive because it was. In May 2016, I moved west and started living a double life: Rio Grande restaurant and hospitality work by day, trying to help build a cannabis startup by night.

It was my first close look at the distance between an industry’s promise and its operating reality. Cannabis was exciting, but it was also logistics, money, access, personalities, documentation, and a thousand little dependencies that could make or break the day.

04 · March 2017

The route stopped being theoretical.

Denver · 2017

In March 2017, my sister-in-law Jessica died unexpectedly. Around the same time, Rio Grande ended as my workplace. Grief and employment instability landed together, and survival became much more literal.

Trimming shifts and dispensary work became part of the bridge. A new Aurora household became chosen family and gave the period some badly needed tenderness and absurdity. By the end of that year, after one short-lived attempt at a more artisanal dispensary, I returned to The Green Solution and back into Asset Protection.

That return matters because it is where cannabis stopped being an adjacent possibility and became an operating environment I would learn from the inside.

05 · The system gets bigger

I kept moving toward the places where the handoffs happened.

The Green Solution · 2017–2020

At The Green Solution, the work crossed processing and packaging, Asset Protection, warehouse and dock operations, product movement, invoicing, and the daily recordkeeping that keeps a regulated operation defensible. I eventually moved into warehouse/dock supervision.

One of the most useful changes I helped implement was RF-scanner-based tracking for warehouse and dispatch workflows. That kind of improvement became a pattern: if the physical movement and the system record were drifting apart, I wanted to know why — and I usually wanted to fix the handoff.

RecordInvoicing, tracking, documentation, reconciliation.
06 · Detours count

Leaving cannabis for a minute made the pattern clearer.

Denver · 2020–2021

Shipping and inventory work outside cannabis reinforced something important: good inventory logic is portable. Orders still need to be tracked. Materials still need to move. Systems still need to agree with the physical world. Teams still inherit whatever the previous team documented — or failed to document.

By the time I returned fully to cannabis inventory work, I was not just thinking about a single store or warehouse. I was thinking about the connective tissue.

07 · The record

I kept ending up where the evidence lives.

Inventory management · 2021–2023

At The Stone, inventory management pulled together the parts of the work I had been circling for years: physical counts, METRC, reconciliations, financial records, system-to-system consistency, audit readiness, and the question underneath all of it — what can the record actually prove?

That question eventually became the through-line behind the tools I build now. I am less interested in making operations look complicated than in making complicated operations legible.

08 · The missing rooms

And then there are exactly two doors I never walked through.

For the record

I have worked around nearly the full regulated supply chain, including inside vertically integrated operations, but I have never directly been the person cultivating the flower or processing concentrates.

Marginal proficiencies: Cultivating Buds — Processing Dabs.

That is not something I want hidden behind a broad “cannabis experience” label. It is useful information. Knowing where direct experience stops is part of knowing what any claim is worth.

09 · Why ClearLine

The gaps sharpen the work. So do all the strange seats in between.

Because I came up through service, security, handoffs, inventory, records, controls, and downstream consequences, I tend to see operations as systems rather than isolated jobs. I want to understand what each team needs from the one before it, what proof survives the handoff, and where unnecessary friction gets mistaken for compliance.

ClearLine is the natural extension of that route: take regulatory complexity, connect it to the real operating floor, and help good-faith operators understand what their process and record actually support.

Know what you know. Name what you do not. Build from the evidence between them.
