The Friction Line

Cannabis was assembled across several regulatory systems. The hard part begins when their requirements meet the people, products, records, money, and jurisdictions running the business.

Cannabis runs through several rulebooks at once. The trouble starts when a decision that satisfies one creates work for everyone else.

Agriculture governs production. Public health governs safety and evidence. Criminal justice establishes legal boundaries. Financial institutions manage economic risk. Consumer regulation governs what reaches the market and how it is represented.

Each agency brought its own terms, forms, and idea of sufficient proof. The operator inherited the job of making them work together. Naturally, nobody included extra hours in the day.

The Oversight Line examined those five forms of oversight and the proof each one demands: show me the crop; show me the evidence; show me the boundary; show me the money; show me the product.

Getting the license was only the opening act.

A cultivation requirement can create a testing consequence. A testing result can determine what inventory may move. An inventory discrepancy can become a reporting issue. A reporting issue can become a licensing problem. A licensing problem can affect banking, insurance, contracting, or expansion.

A requirement can start in cultivation and land on finance’s desk by lunch. The org chart does not contain it.

01 — FEDERAL V. STATE

Who gets to define the market?

Cannabis has spent its modern history operating across overlapping authorities. States have established regulated medical and adult-use markets while federal law has continued to impose its own restrictions and classifications. The federal position itself continues to develop, including recent action concerning FDA-approved marijuana products and certain state-licensed medical marijuana products, alongside a broader federal rescheduling process.

The April 28, 2026 rule puts covered medical marijuana in Schedule III while other marijuana remains Schedule I. The Schedule Line explains that split.

Section 280E generally bars deductions and credits for trafficking in Schedule I or II substances. Treasury says the medical rescheduling action generally removes that bar for qualifying activities; allocation and transition guidance remains a pending item in the 2026–2027 IRS guidance plan. The SAFE Banking Act of 2026 was reintroduced in June; it is proposed legislation, not an enacted banking safe harbor.

Keep the state license handy. Keep reading, too.

Banking, taxation, research, insurance, investment, contracting, intellectual property, employment, and interstate activity can introduce federal considerations that a state license cannot resolve on its own. The Congressional Research Service has specifically examined the complications that the federal-state conflict creates for financial institutions serving state-authorized marijuana businesses.

“Is cannabis legal?” is too broad to run a business on.

Which authority governs this activity, in this location, under these conditions?

Ask it before the purchase order, the transfer, and the sales pitch. Fixing the answer afterward tends to cost more.

02 — RULE V. REALITY

Can the operation execute the requirement?

The rule says to keep a record. Fine. Who enters it, who checks it, and what happens when that person calls out?

A regulation may require a record. Someone has to create it. Someone has to verify it. The organization has to determine where it lives, what evidence supports it, who can change it, and what happens when the process fails.

A testing requirement becomes a scheduling and inventory problem. A security requirement becomes a facility and staffing problem. A reporting requirement becomes a data-management problem. A waste requirement becomes a physical-handling problem. A licensing condition becomes a procedure that has to survive employee turnover, equipment failure, competing priorities, and ordinary human error.

Minnesota's cannabis rules provide a straightforward example: licensed businesses are required to use the state's cannabis monitoring system and comply with requirements governing inventory, tagging, labeling, and tracking.

The state can require a control. Your team still has to make it work on an ordinary Tuesday, with a delivery at the door and two people short.

A policy can describe what should happen. The organization has to make it happen consistently, preserve the record, identify deviations, and maintain a response when the prescribed process cannot be followed.

A binder can document a procedure.

It cannot perform one.

03 — PHYSICAL V. DIGITAL

What happens when the record and the product disagree?

Cannabis traceability increasingly depends on digital records. Plants and packages receive identifiers. Transfers are recorded. Testing results are associated with product. Inventory activity is reported. State systems are designed to maintain an account of cannabis as it moves through the licensed supply chain.

The shelf, unfortunately, does not update itself to match the software.

A package can be mislabeled. A quantity can be entered incorrectly. A transfer can arrive with a discrepancy. A barcode can fail. A system can go offline. Inventory can move before the record catches up.

Now the shelf says one thing and the screen says another. Both cannot be treated as correct just because checking would interrupt the day.

That discrepancy needs reconciliation: establish what happened, correct the record, and keep the evidence.

California's track-and-trace requirements illustrate the scale of that control environment, with rules governing inventory information, transfers, testing, Certificates of Analysis, and related reporting.

The software preserves what was entered.

It does not establish that what was entered was true.

Count it. Check the entry. Record the discrepancy and who corrected it. The software cannot do the honesty part for you.

04 — COMPLIANCE V. COMMERCE

What does compliance require the business to absorb?

Testing costs money. Packaging costs money. Security costs money. Licensing costs money. Recordkeeping requires labor. Training requires time. Software requires implementation and maintenance. Required controls require people to operate them.

Inventory that cannot legally reach the market may also have to be quarantined, remediated, returned, or destroyed, depending on the applicable requirements and circumstances.

These are operating costs whether they appear under “compliance” on the budget or are distributed across production, quality, security, technology, labor, inventory, and finance.

Minnesota's rules, for example, assign cannabis businesses responsibility for costs associated with the state's monitoring system.

A requirement can increase labor. Labor affects margin. Testing can affect throughput. Packaging requirements can affect procurement and production. Inventory controls can affect working capital. Licensing requirements can affect expansion timelines. Product restrictions can reduce the number of products a company can actually bring to market.

Put compliance in the budget before it introduces itself as a surprise expense.

What does satisfying the requirement require the business to build, staff, maintain, finance, document, and carry forward?

05 — REGULATION V. INNOVATION

What happens when the market changes faster than the framework?

Cannabis does not hold still long enough to make classification easy.

Products change. Processing methods change. Cultivation technology changes. Software changes. Formulations change. Packaging changes. Companies develop new ways to manufacture, distribute, market, and sell.

Regulatory systems have to classify those activities using statutes, rules, definitions, authorities, and procedures that may have been written before the particular product or process existed.

California’s rulemaking register separates proposals from effective rules. Multipack goods, group tagging, and track-and-trace updates remain listed as pending. Revised pesticide-testing action levels took effect October 1, 2026. A proposal and an operating requirement should not share the same tense.

The industry can move before the rulebook has settled around the new activity.

Capital can be committed before a classification is clear. Production systems can be built around an interpretation that later changes. A product can be technically manufacturable while its commercial path remains unresolved.

Know what changed before assuming the existing control still fits.

06 — JURISDICTION V. JURISDICTION

What happens when the rules change at the border?

There is no single American cannabis market.

There are state markets operating beside one another, each with its own licensing structures, product requirements, testing standards, packaging rules, tax systems, advertising restrictions, ownership requirements, and approaches to medical and adult-use cannabis.

A process that works in one state may require modification in another. A product approved for sale in one jurisdiction may require different packaging, labeling, testing, documentation, or approval in another.

Minnesota maintains its own packaging and labeling requirements for regulated cannabis products. California maintains its own detailed labeling, packaging, and track-and-trace requirements.

Use the same company standards where they fit. When the state requires something different, “but this is how we do it in Colorado” is background information, not permission.

Expansion adds locations, licenses, vendors, employees, systems, inventory flows, records, and regulatory relationships.

Keep the shared procedures. Mark the state-specific steps clearly enough that a new hire can find them without summoning three managers.

07 — OVERSIGHT V. OVERSIGHT

What happens when everybody is doing their job?

This is where the five lines from The Oversight Line meet the operation.

Agriculture is looking at production. Public health is looking at safety and evidence. Criminal justice is looking at lawful boundaries and enforcement. Financial oversight is looking at economic transparency and risk. Consumer regulation is looking at the product and the person receiving it.

Everyone can be doing their job and still leave the next person with a problem.

A cultivation decision can affect testing. Testing can affect inventory. Inventory can affect sales. Sales affect financial records. Financial records affect reporting. A product claim can raise a public-health question. A licensing issue can affect a banking relationship. A discrepancy that begins in one department can become another department's compliance problem before anyone recognizes the connection.

Packaging and labeling provide another example. Consumer-facing controls can require information, warnings, identifiers, and presentation standards that originate in a regulatory concern but become production, procurement, design, quality, and inventory requirements inside the company.

None of those functions needs to be operating incorrectly. They simply have different jobs.

The compliance problem emerges in the handoff.

One system produces information another system needs. One requirement creates a control somewhere else. One exception moves downstream. A record created for one purpose becomes evidence for another.

Name who sends the record, who receives it, and who checks that it arrived intact. “I thought they had it” is a familiar sentence at the start of an expensive afternoon.

THE OPERATING PROBLEM

The problem is not just the amount of regulation. It is the work required to keep the shelf, the system, the license, and the money telling the same story.

If the package count is wrong, the inventory report is suspect. If the wrong license handled the activity, a perfect count will not fix that. If the money cannot be traced to the sale, the beautiful inventory report has another problem waiting beside it.

And while the team sorts that out, the rules can change. A useful procedure needs someone responsible for noticing.

The useful compliance function sits earlier in the chain. It knows which record proves what happened. It knows who owns the control. It knows which system supplies the evidence. It knows what happens when two records disagree. It knows what changes when a requirement changes.

A testing rule can become an inventory issue. An inventory issue can become a financial issue. A labeling requirement can become a consumer-safety issue. A licensing requirement can become an expansion issue. A federal classification can become a banking issue.

The regulation may have one address.

The consequence usually has several.

FROM OVERSIGHT TO FRICTION

The Oversight Line establishes the systems cannabis has had to answer to in order to become a recognizable, governable industry.

The Friction Line examines what happens when those systems occupy the same operating environment.

Cannabis did not enter a unified regulatory structure designed around the modern industry. It entered through existing systems, each carrying its own definitions, objectives, authorities, records, processes, and expectations.

The industry has spent its evolution making those systems coexist.

That work happens in the gaps: between the license and the operation, between the product and its record, between the rule and the workflow, between the company and the jurisdiction, between the requirement and its cost, and between what the market is doing and what the framework currently describes.

Know the rule. Then follow what it makes the next person do. That is where the missed work tends to hide.
