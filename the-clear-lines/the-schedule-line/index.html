The Schedule Line

Medical and adult-use marijuana no longer occupy the same federal scheduling lane.

Live · October 2026
The line

Medical and adult-use marijuana no longer occupy the same federal scheduling lane.

← Back to the floor

Regulatory review: Checked October 22, 2026. Sources are linked below; gaps in the available updates are marked.

01 — THE SPLIT

The rule effective April 28, 2026 moved covered FDA-approved marijuana products and marijuana subject to a state medical marijuana license to Schedule III. Other marijuana remains Schedule I. A medical label alone does not establish which federal rules cover a transaction.

02 — REGISTRATION STILL MATTERS

The DEA regulatory-actions page provides the medical dispensary registration portal. The rule created an expedited route for state medical license holders; it did not turn every state license into a federal registration. California DCC says applications remain open, but those submitted after June 26 are outside the initial expedition provision.

03 — THE BROADER PROCEEDING

The broader rescheduling hearing is a separate track. In an October 14 order, the judge kept that matter stayed, allowed a reply, reserved a decision on admitting the GAO report, and required a government response to the motion to reopen by October 21. This October 22 review did not verify a later order deciding those motions or lifting the stay. A response deadline does not quietly become a final rule when the clock runs out. The April medical rule remains a separate, effective action.

04 — TAX IS A SEPARATE QUESTION

Section 280E generally bars deductions and credits for trafficking in Schedule I or II substances. Treasury says the medical rescheduling action generally removes that bar for qualifying activities; allocation and transition guidance remains a pending item in the 2026–2027 IRS guidance plan. The SAFE Banking Act of 2026 was reintroduced in June; it is proposed legislation, not an enacted banking safe harbor.

05 — CHECK THE ACTIVITY

Identify the product, license designation, federal registration position, and transaction. Keep medical and adult-use records clear enough to explain their different treatment. Read The Limits Line before treating a scheduling change as permission for a new activity.

ONE COMPANY CAN HAVE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS

The medical/adult-use distinction matters most when a business operates across both. A shared brand or building can make the activity feel unified to a customer. The legal analysis still needs to identify what is being handled, under which authority, and for which transaction. The logo can be the same. The permissions still need checking.

If you handle both medical and adult-use sales, list the actual activities before trying to give the whole company one legal label. Which records establish the license designation? Which products and transactions are involved? Where does a federal registration question arise? Where is the business relying on a future change rather than a present rule? Bring that list to the person advising you. “We sell both” is the beginning of the conversation.

DATES NEED THEIR OWN LABELS

A rule can have a signature date, an announcement date, a publication date, and an effective date. They may appear in different reports without describing different legal events. When an article says “April 2026,” the month may be enough for historical context. When an operator changes a procedure, the exact date and its meaning matter.

This article uses April 28 as the rule’s effective date. It does not use the date of a later headline to move that effective date. The same discipline applies to applications: an application deadline, an expedition window, and an approval are different events. Record what the date governs before deciding what it permits.

LEAVE A RECORD SOMEONE ELSE CAN FOLLOW

You do not need a ceremonial binder. Include the relevant license records, a clear description of the activity, the federal rule or guidance relied upon, the registration position, and a list of unresolved questions. State what has been verified and what still depends on an answer. The next person should be able to check the answer without spending their morning excavating your browser history.

Build the forecast, but label the assumptions. If a benefit depends on guidance, approval, or a future rule, say so beside the number. A classification changed. Now establish what that lets your business do.
